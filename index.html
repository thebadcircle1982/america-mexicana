Mexican and Mexican American life in the United States

The Border Crossed Us

This project challenges the idea that Mexican communities are foreigners in places they’ve called home for generations. In 1848, the United States seized much of what is now the Southwest from Mexico through war. Mexican families were already living there. Many stayed in the same towns, in the same homes. They didn’t cross the border. The border crossed them.

Anti-Mexican discrimination didn’t start with ICE. Our communities have long been treated as outsiders, and today’s immigration crackdown revives that same rhetoric. Federal immigration agents have stopped and detained people in our communities, including brown U.S. citizens. Being brown is not grounds for suspicion. Whether our families were here before the border or arrived yesterday, we don’t have to prove we belong to deserve dignity.

The Border Crossed Us

In 1848, the U.S.–Mexico border moved. Some Mexican families came under U.S. rule without leaving their homes. This story begins there, on lands that were also home to Indigenous nations long before either country existed.

02 / Population by origin

More Than Half

In 2024, people of Mexican origin made up more than half of the U.S. Latino population. That includes people born in the United States as well as immigrants.

03 / Population growth

Growing Across Generations

Most of the growth in the Mexican-origin population between 2000 and 2021 was among people born in the United States.
